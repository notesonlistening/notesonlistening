The First Time

It only happens once.

An opening song of a concert. I recognised it immediately, and yet. The excitement, the suspense, the impatience — it all turned into euphoria. It became something else entirely.

It wasn't my first concert. I knew what to expect — the volume, the lights, the feeling of being somewhere that matters. I knew it was going to be intense. I just didn't know what shape it would take.

What I didn't know was what it would feel like to be in the pit, dancing and singing with strangers who knew every word as well as I did. People I'd never met, who loved the same thing I loved, in the same way.

70,000 people singing at the same time. You know that feeling when a room shifts? It was that. Except the room was a stadium.

That was a first. Not the song. I knew the song by heart, every breath between the lines. Not the concert. The feeling of being part of something that large, that specific, that shared. Now, every time I hear it, I'm back there. In the pit. June 2023. Wanting to go back.

That's what music does. It stops being just sound. It becomes a place.

Everyone has a first. A song, an artist, an album, a moment, something so specific it's no longer just music. It's a core memory, a place you return to without deciding to.

That's what Notes on LISTENING is about. The moments music stops being background.

Essay

A promise

MOVED

On waiting, wanting, and the chemistry of joy.

What happens in your brain when your favourite artist drops a new album?

I wasn't supposed to listen to it there.

The album had just dropped and I couldn't wait, not even long enough to get home. I was listening to it as a background sound, half-distracted, half-present. Not how you're supposed to hear something you've been waiting for. Not how I wanted to experience it. But I was too impatient. It was the first time I had ever listened to a new album this way, sharing something close to me with family.

I knew, even before pressing play, that first single I'd had on repeat two months prior was already telling me I was going to love what was coming. But I couldn't give it what it deserved.

Not there, not like that.

A few hours later, I put it on alone, headphones on and volume up. I listened to it and danced, through my dishes, my cleaning, my bedtime routine. Even in bed, ready to sleep, I was still blasting the album and trying to learn the lyrics. I couldn't stop listening. It made me happy in a way I'd never imagined an album could. I wanted to share it and I sent voice messages at midnight to my friends expressing how happy I was about hearing his music.

I was already thinking about the next morning. About pressing play again, from the beginning. That thought alone made me euphoric.

It was the first time a new album from a favourite artist had moved me so completely. And somewhere in the middle of all that dancing, I started wondering why and how, and what happens.

What I didn't know at the time was that it was a constructed anticipation. Not mine. A first single. Then silence. Then a tracklist, just titles, no music. A cover, a world tour for the album without revealing it. A release date circled on a calendar. It's a sequence. Designed to create exactly what I felt.

Which raises a question I'm still sitting with: does understanding the mechanism make the feeling less real? It doesn't. The joy was real and worth it.

So I went looking. Just to understand what it was, not to prove it wrong.

Anticipating music activates the same reward circuit in the brain as basic needs, and, as surprising as it may seem, cocaine and amphetamines. Music, as abstract as it is, can have the same effect in the brain as chocolate, as drugs.

The pleasure of listening is warm, immediate. It arrives and then it ends. The anticipation is different, it's restless. You want something you don't have yet. It almost aches.

Anticipation and listening are not the same thing in the brain either. The brain releases dopamine in two distinct waves when we listen to music we love. The first wave happens during the anticipation of an emotionally intense passage, the second one when it arrives. Two separate anatomical pathways. Two separate floods of dopamine.

So I wasn't just excited. My brain was chemically rewarding me for wanting something I didn't have yet.

But the anticipation didn't end when the album started. It just changed scale. Every time a new track began, there was that split second of not knowing. A familiar voice, a universe I already loved, but this song, I had never heard it. That tension between the known and the unknown is where the brain is buzzing.

I was impatient through the whole thing. Not because I wasn't enjoying it. Because I wanted more of it before it was gone.

There's something else at play here. It wasn't just any album. It was this artist. His album.

I've been listening to him for about five years. And suddenly I couldn't stop. I went deeper and deeper, and at some point he became my favourite artist. The one with no skips. The one I've listened to on repeat for months. The one whose voice I could recognise from the first few seconds. The one I knew, before a single note of this album played, that I was going to love.

That certainty is not just emotional. It's neurological. When you've spent years with an artist, your brain doesn't just enjoy the music. It learns. The intensity of this response varies from person to person, which means if you've ever seen someone cry at a song and wondered what was happening, you're just wired differently.

A favourite artist isn't just a preference.
It's a promise.

Which is also why the disappointment can be so devastating when it doesn't deliver. When a predicted reward doesn't arrive, dopamine doesn't just stay flat. It drops.

The bigger the expectation, the bigger the fall.

This is why fans react so strongly when a favourite artist changes direction. It's a brain that built a very precise prediction, based on years of listening, years of feeling, and memories, and they received something it didn't anticipate. The disappointment is chemical, the emotion is real. The intensity of the joy and the intensity of the disappointment come from the same place.

There's a moment when the happiness becomes too big to keep to yourself.

The dancing was mine. Personal and private, alone in my room, moving to music no one else could hear. That was my own way of holding it, enjoying it, discovering it. But at some point even that wasn't enough. I needed to tell someone, close friends and family. Not because I wanted them to feel the same thing. I knew they wouldn't. Most of them won't ever listen to the full album. Some of them had no idea the album had even dropped that day. And that's okay.

That's what sharing happiness is, I think. It's not about validation. It's not about converting anyone.

It's just that joy, at a certain intensity, needs a witness.

Sharing a positive experience with someone you love doesn't just feel good. It reactivates the experience neurologically. You don't just remember the joy. You relive it. You get hit by another wave of dopamine, triggered by the act of telling someone.

The dopamine hit didn't stop when the album ended. The dancing and the voice notes at midnight kept going, and it started all over again the day after. My brain was looking for new ways to feel it or extend it. Just a few minutes longer.

I didn't know any of this that night. I still know that the joy I felt that night was real. And I still feel it. I was just dancing. I still dance to it. All of it was genuine.

And I think when you understand why something moves you, it doesn't move you less. It moves you differently.

P.S. The album was Kiss all the time, Disco occasionally.

Deep Cut

It hadn't got a past

YOUR BRAIN ON MUSIC

There is always a first listen, but nothing is ever quite like the first.

A first listen is not looking for a timestamp, or anticipating the bridge or your favourite part. It's discovering, it's learning the song. From the second listen, you know what to expect, you already know the song, so you start looking for the details, consciously or unconsciously. The harmonies, the bass line, the back vocals.

You'll never get back to the state of hearing it for the first time. You associate it with the moment you heard it for the first time. It's context-dependent memory. It's a Music-Evoked Autobiographical Memory, a MEAM.

The song and the moment encode together. They become inseparable.

The neurological pathways reactivate that exact same moment the next time you listen to that specific song. You're brought back to the emotional state of that time. It's not a memory anymore, it's replaying the moment.

The song will never exist in that same state again. You can never get that back.

It hadn't got a past, not with you.

You created that past, and therefore that association, by hearing it for the first time and noticing it. That link will forever hold. But it will not always come back. Not every time.

Not knowing it is the first time creates an innocence in hearing the song. As soon as we start acknowledging it, it changes.

Every first listen is already ending before you even realise it's happening.

The song remains unchanged. The meaning does not. It is not only memories that are recalled, but past versions of ourselves. Sometimes, years later, a song reveals something that was there all along, waiting to be recognised.

MEAMs

Music-Evoked Autobiographical Memories

The neurological pathway that links a song to the exact emotional state of the first time you heard it.

Not a memory, a replay.

04
The Angle Nobody Tells
1 9 7 7

Fleetwood Mac opened a door for me. This is what I found behind it.

Mick has his name on every album, every credit. But nobody sees it. Fleetwood Mac was named after him. Peter Green wanted Mick and John to keep the name, whatever happened next. The group carries it from the very first day.

He is the origin. The constant. They all leave or get fired. He stays and rebuilds. Every time. At some point he looks around and realises he's the only one left from the beginning. Fleetwood Mac wouldn't exist without him. Nobody says that. He's not Stevie Nicks or Lindsey Buckingham. And yet.

Rumours has its own story. We know it. A group of five, four of them going through heartbreaks with each other. That's the drama Rumours is. Mick has already lived his own betrayal and divorce, one that came before the album, a pain too early to be part of the narrative.

And so while the others write songs about each other, he holds the group together. As a drummer. As the founder. His pain doesn't take any space. There is none for him.

Drums don't carry melody or lyrics. They carry rhythm. The most essential part of a song.

He carries the name of the group. The group has no song for him on the most personal album they ever made.

Rumours — Fleetwood Mac — February 1977
